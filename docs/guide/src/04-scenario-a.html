Scenario A · port 8080

sized: the good failure

A correctly sized JVM with a heap leak. This is how memory failures should happen: the JVM notices first, writes its evidence and stays in control. You will also learn the full investigation method here, from symptom to the line of code.

A1

Configuration and verdict

compose: sized
mem_limit: 1g      memswap_limit: 1g
JAVA_OPTS: "-XX:MaxRAMPercentage=60 -XX:MaxDirectMemorySize=96m"
Technically

The heap ceiling is derived from the limit (60 % of 1 GiB = 614 MiB), leaving ~410 MiB for everything else. Measured non-heap footprint at idle: ~74–79 MiB. Direct memory is capped at 96 MiB, so it can add at most that. Projected peak with a full heap and full direct buffers: 614 + 74 + 95 = 784 MiB (76 % of the limit), so the verdict is SIZED (real verdict). Both JVM-enforced ceilings sit below the kernel's.

Like I'm five

The tenant's storage-room rule (614) plus the furniture (79) fits comfortably in the apartment (1024). If the storage room ever fills, the tenant will notice it first, long before the landlord would.

Purpose

This is the reference configuration: the one that makes a heap leak diagnosable. Everything else in the guide is a deviation from it.

A2

Healthy churn: lots of allocation, no growth

preset "Healthy churn"

200 requests/s × 256 KiB, 0 % retained, so 50 MiB/s of allocation and nothing kept.

Technically
  1. Each request allocates a byte[262144] and a SessionContext inside the load-engine thread's TLH, in the nursery's allocate space. This is a fast bump-pointer allocation with no lock.
  2. When allocate is exhausted, an allocation failure triggers a scavenge. The JVM pauses Java threads briefly, copies the objects still reachable (only the in-flight request) to survivor, and treats the whole of allocate as empty again. Cost scales with live objects, not with garbage. That is why throwing away 50 MiB/s is cheap.
  3. GC notifications report "after" occupancy, and GcWatcher's live set stays flat.
Like I'm five

Lots of parcels arrive, get opened and the wrapping goes in the bin by the door. The cleaner empties that bin many times a minute. It's busy, but the room never gets fuller.

Purpose

To calibrate your eye. High allocation and frequent GC are not a leak. Only a rising floor is. Many false leak reports come from people watching heap usage oscillate.

A3

The leak begins

preset "Slow leak"

20 requests/s × 128 KiB × 10 % retained ≈ 0.25 MiB/s ≈ 15 MiB/min of permanent growth.

Technically
  1. On 10 % of requests, CustomerSessionCache.remember(ctx) puts the context in a static ConcurrentHashMap. Because the key is a fresh UUID, the lookup before it never hits.
  2. At the next scavenge, the context and its byte[] are reachable (static field → map → node → context → array), so they are copied to survivor. After surviving enough scavenges they are tenured.
  3. Tenure fills slowly. When it needs space, a global GC marks reachable objects across the whole heap. The cached contexts are all marked live and none can be freed. The post-global-GC occupancy is higher than last time.
  4. If free space after GC falls below -Xminf, OpenJ9 expands the heap (committed grows towards 614 MiB). RSS grows with it as new pages are touched.
Like I'm five

One in ten parcels gets put in a basket that's tied to the wall. The cleaner moves the basket to the back room because it's clearly still wanted, and it is never emptied. After every full clean the apartment is a little fuller than after the previous one.

Purpose

To show that a leak is quiet at first. Nothing errors and latency is unchanged. Only the trend reveals it, which is why trend-based monitoring matters.

A4

Recognising the trend

Technically · four independent instruments
  • UI live signal: linear regression of the after-GC series over 90 s. Above 2 MiB/min it reports a heap leak signature and estimates time to the limit.
  • Verbose GC log: each global cycle's <mem-info> after-collection value rises. IBM GCMV plots "used heap after global collection".
  • Prometheus: deriv(leaklab_heap_live_after_gc_bytes[10m]) > 0.
  • Class histogram (JDK image): jcmd 1 GC.class_histogram twice; io.leaklab.leak.SessionContext count grows with total requests served.
Like I'm five

Four different people confirm the same thing: the scoreboard, the cleaner's diary, the building's monthly report, and someone who counted the boxes twice. When they agree, you know it's hoarding and not just a busy day.

Architect's note

Decide at design time which of these instruments exist in production, and that someone will look at them. Typically that means the GC log on a volume (always), Prometheus with a slope alert (always), and the ability to take a histogram or dump (runbook).

A5

Taking heap dumps D1 and D2

button "Heap dump" · POST /api/dumps/heap
Technically · what happens inside the JVM
  1. DumpService calls com.ibm.jvm.Dump.heapDumpToFile("/dumps/heapdump.sized.….phd").
  2. The JVM requests exclusive VM access: every Java thread is brought to a safe point and paused (stop-the-world).
  3. The heap is made walkable. Depending on the agent's request options this can include a collection and compaction, so the dump reflects live objects.
  4. The dumper walks every object and writes PHD records (class, size, address, outgoing references) to disk, then releases exclusive access. Threads resume.

Take D1 after warm-up, run the leak for a fixed interval (for example 4 minutes), then take D2. Two dumps let you see growth; one dump only shows size.

Like I'm five

Everyone in the apartment freezes like statues while the photographer photographs every box and every string. Then everyone carries on. A week later the photographer comes back. Comparing the two albums shows what keeps piling up.

In the lab · real numbers

A PHD of a ~600 MiB heap full of byte[] payloads was 2.7 MiB. PHD stores arrays without their contents, so it records "a byte array of 524 288 bytes at address X" but not the bytes. The guardian's PHD in Scenario D took 249 ms to write.

Pitfalls
  • The pause can last seconds on big heaps. If the liveness probe times out meanwhile, the kubelet SIGKILLs the pod mid-dump: evidence lost, and a death that looks like an OOMKill.
  • Dumps contain production data (here: customer IDs and session payloads). Handle them like a database export.

Architect's note · the fix pattern

Replace the map with a bounded, expiring cache (for example Caffeine maximumSize(…).expireAfterWrite(…)), keyed by a stable idempotency key. Expose its size as a metric. As a design-review rule, every cache or registry should state its bound and its eviction policy.

A7

Driving it into OutOfMemoryError

preset "Aggressive leak"

100 requests/s × 512 KiB × 40 % retained = 20 MiB/s of leak plus 30 MiB/s of churn. 614 MiB ÷ 20 MiB/s ≈ 31 s. The real run reached OOM after ~31 s.

Technically
  1. The heap expands until committed = -Xmx. It cannot grow further.
  2. Global GCs become frequent and recover almost nothing, because nearly everything is live. The GC chart shows a growing global share and overhead.
  3. An allocation of 512 KiB fails. OpenJ9 runs a full global collection with compaction as a last attempt. It still fails.
  4. The JVM raises the systhrow event for java/lang/OutOfMemoryError. Before the exception reaches any Java code, the dump agents run (A8).
  5. The OutOfMemoryError: Java heap space is thrown in the allocating thread, here leaklab-load-engine. Other threads that allocate at that moment (HTTP workers) may also get OOMs.
  6. LoadEngine catches it, releases the parachute, pauses the load and writes a CRITICAL journal entry. The pause is a lab choice: the leaked memory is still full, and more load would only raise more OOMs. Set After an OutOfMemoryError to Keep running to see what a real service gets instead: OOMs every tick and a tenth of the load served. In the lab, HotSpot (Red Hat 21) kept answering for minutes, while OpenJ9 (Semeru 17) lost its HTTP threads to OOMs within 90 s and became a zombie that only a restart clears. That is why production uses -XX:+ExitOnOutOfMemoryError.
Like I'm five

The storage room reaches its size limit. The tenant asks for one last full clean, and nothing can go, because everything is tied to the wall. The tenant says "storage room full!". The automatic camera takes its photos, then the tenant stops accepting parcels.

Purpose

To see the controlled failure end to end, and to see that the error may hit an innocent thread. The stack trace of an OOM tells you who asked for memory last, not who leaked it. Only the dump tells you that.

A9

Recovery, and why production should not recover

button "Release retained"
Technically

"Release" clears the map, calls System.gc() and re-arms the parachute. Real result: 577 MiB released; heap 66 MiB, live set 37 MiB a few seconds later. The service carried on.

The first version of LeakLab did not survive. After the OOM, the HTTP layer kept failing with:

ERROR [io.ve.ex.we.RoutingContext] Unhandled exception in router:
java.lang.BootstrapMethodError: java.lang.OutOfMemoryError: Java heap space

Lambdas and string concatenation use invokedynamic call sites, which are linked on first use. If that linkage fails (here, because of the OOM), the JVM specification makes the failure permanent for that call site. Every later call rethrows the same error, even after memory has been freed. The fix in the lab has two parts: a 24 MiB parachute array that is dropped on OOM to give the control plane room to work, and a warm-up at boot that links the telemetry call sites while memory is plentiful.

Like I'm five

When the storage room was completely full, a worker tried to learn a new task and failed. Now they believe for ever that they "can't do that task", even after the room has been cleared. The lab cheats by keeping a small empty emergency corner, and by having workers practise every task on day one.

Architect's note

In production, prefer fail fast: -XX:+ExitOnOutOfMemoryError. The dump agents run first, then the process exits, and Kubernetes starts a clean instance. A half-broken JVM that stays "up" and passes liveness checks is worse than a restart.

A10

Variant: the direct-buffer leak

target "Direct"
Technically

ByteBuffer.allocateDirect takes native memory, counted against MaxDirectMemorySize (96 MiB here). A Cleaner frees it when the small DirectByteBuffer on the heap is collected; retained ones never are, so at 96 MiB the next allocation throws OutOfMemoryError: Cannot reserve … bytes of direct buffer memory and the JVM lives on. java.nio.Bits throws it, not the VM, so the default OOM agents don't fire (verified; nor does HotSpot's flag). The image's events=throw agent writes the dumps; its msg_filter=*direct* stops a re-thrown heap OOM from using up its one shot. Uncapped, the container would be killed first: that is Scenario C.

Like I'm five

Crates are stored outside the storage room, but the tenant keeps a tally with its own limit of 96. When the tally is full, the tenant raises the alarm themselves, before the landlord notices.

Purpose

It shows the general principle: caps turn silent kills into diagnosable errors. Whenever a native consumer can be capped inside the JVM, cap it.

Scenario A · key takeaways
  • Sizing by percentage of the container limit, with real headroom, makes the JVM the first to fail.
  • The live set after GC is the leak indicator. Heap usage and GC frequency alone are not.
  • Investigate with at least two dumps: propose, locate, prove, confirm, explain.
  • OpenJ9 writes evidence on OOM by default, provided /dumps is a writable, persistent volume.
  • After OOM, exit and restart. Don't limp on.

A · Forensics readiness: what must be in place to diagnose a heap leak

A heap leak can only be investigated with evidence that was set up to exist before the leak. Most items below are JVM flags or pod-spec settings, and those only take effect after a restart. If you start configuring them during an incident, the restart wipes the leaked state you wanted to study. That's why they belong in the platform standard, not the runbook.
